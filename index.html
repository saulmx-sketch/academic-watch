Amanda Úrsula Torres Freyermuth

Secuencia, núm. 126 · publicado 2026-10-26

Resumen

Este artículo narra el conflicto por las tierras de Castarricas entre los indígenas del pueblo de Zapaluta y Manuel José de Rojas, que tuvo lugar entre finales del siglo XVIII y 1830, como un caso ejemplar de las consecuencias del constitucionalismo en Chiapas. A partir del examen del expediente judicial se reconstruyó el prolongado enfrentamiento territorial que confrontó dos concepciones del derecho: una basada en la tradición y la memoria colectiva, y otra sustentada en la propiedad exclusiva, de tendencia liberal. Masculinidades en transformación: Debates y tensiones de género en México

Nelson Florez Vaquiro

Revista Interdisciplinaria de Estudios de Género de El Colegio de México, vol. 12, pp. 1-8 · publicado 2026-11-09

Resumen

Hombres y relaciones de género en México, de Olga Lorena Rojas, ofrece una revisión amplia de la investigación social sobre las masculinidades y las relaciones de género en el país. Esta reseña examina los cinco ejes que estructuran la obra: el marco conceptual sobre masculinidades y trayectorias de vida, la sexualidad, la proveeduría económica, el trabajo doméstico y de cuidados, y la relación entre el trabajo remunerado de las mujeres y la violencia masculina en el hogar. Conjuring Columbia: Phillis Wheatley’s Goddess, Miranda’s Colombia, and Hemispheric Limits on Imagining Armed People of Color, 1775–1807

Lina Del Castillo

The Americas: A Quarterly Review of Latin American History, pp. 1-33 · publicado 2026-11-09

Resumen

Why is “Colombia,” in South America, not spelled “Columbia”? And what can the largely forgotten history behind this orthographic divergence reveal? Two remarkable figures from the Age of Revolutions, Phillis Wheatley and Francisco de Miranda, are rarely considered together to answer these questions. After publishing her first book of poetry in 1773, Wheatley gained freedom from slavery and dedicated a poem to General George Washington, who had it published in 1776. Her poem was the first to fully personify through mythic allegory the golden-haired goddess “Columbia,” who subtly called in armies (inclusive of people of color) to win freedom from Britannia. Wheatley’s poetic moves galvanized a US audience. By 1784, when Miranda claimed to have met Wheatley in Boston, Columbia was everywhere, yet few attributed the goddess to Wheatley’s poetic prowess, and most ignored Wheatley’s careful deployment of Columbia’s inclusive army. Realizing “Columbia’s” value as shorthand for New World independence, Miranda plucked “Columbia” from the North American context and adjusted her name to Spanish-language spelling conventions. Miranda’s linguistic move, given “Columbia’s” already existing popularity, helped him gain transatlantic notoriety for “Colombian” independence from Spain. Delving into eighteenth–nineteenth-century poetry, correspondence, and print culture demonstrates how Wheatley’s “Columbia” inspired Miranda’s “Colombia,” and, in turn, how this poetic geopolitical vision helped make thinkable transatlantic imaginaries of freedom. For both Wheatley and Miranda, their Columbian/Colombian imaginaries included people of color among the ranks of her liberating armies, yet their calls for inclusion required careful calibration (and at times erasure).

New Goods and New Habits: Cleanliness as a Class Marker and the Making of the Peasant Homemaker, Sweden 1850–1910

Marie Ulväng

Gender & History · publicado 2026-11-06

Resumen

The article examines what cleanliness, that is, hygiene and tidiness, meant for landowning farm households in the county of Härjedalen in the inland of northern Sweden from the 1860s to the 1910s. The period is characterised by a growing understanding of bacteria and of how infectious diseases could be prevented through improved hygiene, leading towards a ‘germ panic’. In Härjedalen, the industrialisation of forestry led many farm households to sell their homesteads, leaving landowning farm households a small but influential group in local society. The study shows that wealthier farm households improved their houses and adopted bourgeois customs and attitudes to cleanliness to distinguish themselves from the landless peasantry. The shift in the main source of income from livestock farming to forestry altered the dynamics of men's and women's tasks and responsibilities, enabling wives to devote more time to housework. The increase in domestic work and the reallocation of labour were not driven by fear of bacteria or by social pressure from above, but by cleanliness and housewifery as markers of social class. Spacious and well‐kept homes were not an option for the areas’ workers, crofters and tenants.

Elizabeth Banks

Gender & History · publicado 2026-11-06

Resumen

The 1974 declaration of the New International Economic Order (NIEO), which came just ahead of the UN's International Women's Year (IWY) in 1975, attempted a major renegotiation of global inequality without reference to gender. Through the rest of the 1970s, women at the African Training and Research Centre for Women (ATRCW) in Addis Ababa, itself an initiative of the IWY, leveraged the power of the NIEO to support their undervalued work on African women's economic lives. Bat sacrifice as a significant element in Maya earth rituals

James E. Brady

Social Compass · publicado 2026-11-05

Resumen

Maya religion has a heavily terrestrial focus, growing out of the Amerindian belief in Earth as an all-powerful, sacred, and animate entity. Features that penetrate Earth, particularly caves, are revered as sacred landmarks because many of their most important deities are believed to reside in caves. Levy-Bruhl noted that sacred landmarks are not single features but complexes that include the animal species that inhabit those places. Thus, bats were important actors in the Maya’s interaction with their deities. Bat remains appear in several specific ritual contexts. In this article, the author presents evidence to suggest that bats may have accompanied the dead in burials. More importantly, a reanalysis of cave data suggests that the sacrifice of bats may have been a frequent occurrence in caves where bats functioned as messengers to the deities.

Why can’t Muslims in Indonesia say ‘Merry Christmas’ in public? Dual integralism and moral sovereignty

Justito Adiprasetio

Social Compass · publicado 2026-11-05

Resumen

This article examines the recurring controversy over whether Muslims, who form the demographic majority in Indonesia, may say ‘Merry Christmas’, analyzing it as a symptom of dual integralism, the entangled authority of state and Islamic institutions in regulating moral conduct. Through a Foucauldian genealogical lens, the study traces how symbolic disputes are shaped by state integralism, rooted in the homogenizing logic of Pancasila, and Islamic integralism, which seeks to assert religious norms in public life. By analyzing key moments from the 1945 Jakarta Charter debates to the Majelis Ulama Indonesia (Indonesia Ulama Council) fatwas issued in 1981 and 2024, the article shows that moral authority in Indonesia is both contested and co-produced across state and religious institutions. The greeting controversy operates as a proxy for broader struggles over legitimacy and normative control. Within this dual structure, everyday symbolic acts become flashpoints through which Indonesia’s moral and civic boundaries are repeatedly negotiated.

Isabella Vergara C.

Latin American Research Review, pp. 1-24 · publicado 2026-11-06

Resumen

Este artículo propone una lectura de tres obras de Verónica Gerber Bicecci a partir de la dimensión visual y sonora del silencio, en diálogo con los estudios contemporáneos sobre el sonido, en particular los aportes de Ana María Ochoa Gautier, David Toop y Jean-Luc Nancy, y con una redefinición crítica del collage basada en el trabajo reciente de Freya Gowrley, Fragmentary Forms (2024). Más que un medio o un género, el collage se concibe aquí como una práctica de pensamiento material que desarma la genealogía canónica del papier collé cubista y se plantea como una forma de ensamblaje transhistórica y transmedial. La propuesta central del artículo es pensar el silencio como una materia activa, una zona intersticial que remite a una apertura perceptiva donde las citas, los cortes y los residuos retoman agencia. A partir de un recorrido por una serie visual del silencio en la obra de Gerber Bicecci, el texto analiza dos momentos en los que el silencio se despatriarcaliza al habitar otras materias: Mujeres polilla (2018) y otro día… (poemas sintéticos) (2023). En ambas, el uso de la acetona, el polvo y el residuo activa una poética del emborronamiento que subvierte las oposiciones entre forma y contenido, entre lo decible y lo indecible. El artículo concluye con un análisis de Centón pétreo (2021), donde el collage de citas visuales y literarias fragmentadas dramatiza un silencio que reverbera. En todos los casos, el collage permite visibilizar un tipo de silencio que desborda su definición convencional y opera como una fractura de la forma: un corte material y discursivo que interpela los regímenes de género, las políticas de archivo y las formas de escucha. Así, el silencio deviene en acto indisciplinado que, desde su mínima y fragmentaria materialidad, ensaya otras formas de imaginación política.

Gender and Violence Between the Home and the Street: The Narrative Construction of the Public/Private Boundary in Brazil

Natália Otto, Roberta S. Pamplona, Luisa Farah Schwartzman

Latin American Research Review, pp. 1-18 · publicado 2026-11-06

Resumen

Latin American scholars have problematized the binary between private gender-based violence and public urban violence, highlighting the interconnection of state, urban, and gendered violence. Yet cultural notions of the public and private continue to inform the “gendering” of violence. In this article, we examine how police officers who investigate murders of women (thirty-six inquiries and nine interviews) and girls incarcerated in a juvenile detention center (twenty-two interviews) in Brazil narrate violence through a differential gendering of the categories of the “home” and the “street.” We find that the street is framed as a space of genderless, (drug) market-related, and expected instrumental violence, and the home is a space of gendered and unexpected emotional violence. This framing reduces gender violence to emotional disputes within the family and naturalizes violence in the “street,” portraying it as unavoidable, thus obscuring and legitimating structural state and gender violence against women.

Resumen

A detailed examination of words that can be attributed to Quingnam, the ancient language of Chimor, in Peru’s North Coast, reveals a marked preponderance of sea-related terms. The words of possible Quingnam origin that we have identified refer to activities such as fishing and totora cultivation and make meticulous distinctions between animal and plant species populating the coast. Negotiating Intimate Boundaries: Gender, Sexuality, and Migration in 1960s West Germany

Elisabeth Kimmerle

Gender & History · publicado 2026-11-05

Resumen

In the 1960s, the children of unmarried women migrant workers in West Germany became a transnational political case that occupied federal and state authorities, social workers, and consulates. Reproductive matters embodied a state of vulnerability for migrant women who not only feared stigmatization in their home countries, but also faced deportation if they did not give up their children for adoption and had to receive welfare benefits. This article explores the ways in which gender and sexuality deeply shaped labor migration and, as a result, politicized migrants’ private and intimate relations. It argues that pregnancy among unmarried migrant women became a site of conflicting interests of the West German and Turkish migration regimes. Within the constrained space between those two migration regimes, migrant women developed precarious forms of agency such as transnational adoption and abortion. Drawing on archival sources, oral history interviews, and Turkish newspaper reports, the article aims to contribute to the historiography of migration from a gender‐historical perspective that allows for a nuanced understanding of the complex ways in which intimate modes became political points of contention.

Breaking the Ice: Contributions and Challenges for Andean Glaciological Research Led by Women

Ana Huaico-Malhue, Edilia Jaque, Ana Guadalupe Hernández-Duarte, Marion San Juan Díaz, Mariajosé Herrera-Ossandón, Elizabet Lizama, Beatriz Cid Aguayo

Latin American Research Review, pp. 1-22 · publicado 2026-11-02

Resumen

This study examines the participation of women in Andean glaciology over the past four decades, combining a bibliometric analysis of 204 peer-reviewed articles (1983–2022) and a survey of female first authors (2018–2022). Results show a steady increase in women-led publications, rising from 8 percent in the 1980s–1990s to 30 percent in the past decade. However, structural barriers, including unequal access to funding, resistance to female leadership, and the disproportionate burden of caregiving, continue to limit gender equity in the field. Women-led studies are concentrated in Argentina and Chile, while countries like Colombia, Bolivia, and Venezuela remain underrepresented. The methods most commonly used by female researchers are fieldwork and remote sensing, which reflects limited access to laboratory-based approaches. Survey responses highlight both the motivations (scientific interest, conservation) and challenges (discrimination, lack of recognition, work-life imbalance) faced by women scientists. The findings reveal persistent epistemic and territorial asymmetries, especially in the context of international research led by Global North institutions. The study underscores the need for inclusive policies that go beyond formal representation, supporting equitable research leadership and collaboration. Resumen
